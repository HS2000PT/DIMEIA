\chapter{Methods and Materials}
\label{chap:methods}

This chapter answers two questions: how is the system built, and how is it judged? It covers the approach,
the data (in a reproducible data card), the techniques behind each component, the responsible-AI
commitments, and the evaluation design, which was fixed before any experiment was run. One idea runs
through all of it: keep things as simple as they can defensibly be, so that when two options perform about
the same, the more transparent one wins. InvestiGator itself is described in Chapter~\ref{chap:investigator}; the
experiments are reported in Chapter~\ref{chap:casestudies}.

\section{Approach and Methodology}
\label{sec:met_approach}

\emph{How was the system built?} Incrementally, end to end. Rather than finishing each component in
isolation, a thin slice was built and proven first, from data acquisition to a delivered alert, so that
integration risk surfaced early. Each component then started in its simplest defensible form and grew only
where the extra complexity earned its place. This is what Artificial Intelligence \emph{engineering} means
here: the building blocks already exist in the literature (Chapter~\ref{chap:sota}), so the contribution is
their disciplined integration, application and critical evaluation, not a new algorithm.

Two habits follow. First, every component is defined by its \emph{responsibility} and its \emph{interface},
not by a particular implementation, which keeps it testable and swappable (one embedding model for another,
say). Second, the project's non-negotiable constraints, only free and reproducible resources, the US
market, transparency, and no prediction or trading, filter the design space before any performance
question.

\label{sec:met_data}

\emph{What data does the system use?} Two clearly separated layers that share one schema, so they are
directly comparable (Figure~\ref{fig:data_layers}): a \textbf{historical} layer, built offline, that
supplies the precedents, and a \textbf{live} layer that supplies real-time prices and news. The split is
deliberate: the historical layer must be rich and stable, the live layer timely and cheap.

\subsection{Preprocessing and Event Alignment}
\label{sec:met_data_prep}

\emph{How is a news item turned into a historical case?} In three steps. First, the news file is read in a
stream and filtered to the chosen tickers and window, so only the relevant subset is materialised. Second,
each item is normalised to the common schema (date, ticker, headline). Third, each item is aligned to the
market and its impact measured. The alignment rule matters for honesty: the event day is the first trading
day \emph{on or after} the news date, and impact is measured from that day's \emph{close} onward. Measuring
from the close, not the open, avoids crediting a move already in the opening price, and measuring strictly
forward enforces the no-lookahead guarantee of Section~\ref{sec:met_evaluation}. Items with no aligned
prices, or whose window runs past the data, are dropped rather than imputed. Algorithm~\ref{alg:kbbuild}
states the build.

\begin{algorithm}[ht]
\caption{Knowledge-base construction with event alignment.}
\label{alg:kbbuild}
\begin{algorithmic}[1]
\Require news items $\{(d_i,c_i,h_i)\}$; daily close prices $P_c$ per ticker; horizons $H=\{1,3,5\}$
\Ensure knowledge base $\mathcal{K}$ of cases $(d,c,h,\mathbf{e},\text{impact})$
\State $\mathcal{K} \gets \emptyset$
\For{each news item $(d_i,c_i,h_i)$}
  \State $e_i \gets$ first trading day $\ge d_i$ in $P_{c_i}$ \Comment{event alignment}
  \If{$e_i$ exists}
    \State $\mathbf{e}_i \gets \mathrm{embed}(h_i)$ \Comment{Sentence-BERT}
    \For{$\tau \in H$}
      \State $\text{impact}_i[\tau] \gets$ return from close($e_i$) to close($e_i{+}\tau$), or \textsc{n/a} if unavailable
    \EndFor
    \State $\mathcal{K} \gets \mathcal{K} \cup \{(d_i,c_i,h_i,\mathbf{e}_i,\text{impact}_i)\}$
  \EndIf
\EndFor
\State \Return $\mathcal{K}$
\end{algorithmic}
\end{algorithm}

\subsection{Live Layer and Evaluation Dataset}
\label{sec:met_data_live}

The live layer provides real-time prices from a free market-data service (with a free fallback) and news
from a free company-news service and RSS feeds. Free news is deliberately \emph{not} used to build the
historical layer, because free tiers cover only a short, recent, delayed window; but it is fine as the live
trigger and as a source of \emph{real} news for a first evaluation. For that, a set of $3{,}714$ real
headlines for the fifteen tickers was collected from the free company-news service (the most recent months
available), and is used for the retrieval study in Chapter~\ref{chap:casestudies}. This corpus is real but
recent, which is why the full multi-year FNSPID build remains the richer source, identified as future work.

\subsection{Data Governance and Attribution}
\label{sec:met_data_gov}

Two governance rules apply to both layers. First, large data and models are never versioned: they are
recreated by the ingestion process, and only small illustrative samples are kept under version control.
Second, third-party news text is not republished: the dataset is cited and attributed as required by its
Creative Commons BY-SA 4.0 licence \autocite{dong2024fnspid}, and only minimal headline excerpts appear
in this document.

\section{Models and Techniques}
\label{sec:met_techniques}

Three techniques do the work: a detector, a retrieval-and-impact engine, and an explanation step. Each is
introduced by what it is \emph{for}, then how it works.

\subsection{Statistical Anomaly Detection}
\label{sec:met_anomaly}

\emph{What it is for:} to flag a daily move that is unusual \emph{for that stock}, not just large in
absolute terms. \emph{How it works:} let $r_t$ be the day's (log-)return and $\mu_t,\sigma_t$ the mean and
standard deviation of returns over a trailing window of $w$ days ending strictly before $t$. The day is
flagged when the absolute $z$-score exceeds a threshold $k$:
\begin{equation}
z_t = \frac{r_t - \mu_t}{\sigma_t}, \qquad \text{anomaly} \iff |z_t| > k .
\label{eq:zscore}
\end{equation}
This is the statistical family of detectors \autocite{chandola2009anomaly}, chosen for transparency: the
investor can be shown exactly how many standard deviations the move was. More expressive detectors exist,
Isolation Forest \autocite{liu2008isolation} or deep models \autocite{pang2021deep}, but their opacity is
not warranted for a single return series explained to a non-expert (Chapter~\ref{chap:sota}). The window
$w$ and threshold $k$ are explicit choices, examined by ablation in Chapter~\ref{chap:casestudies}.
Algorithm~\ref{alg:zscore} states the rule; the window for day $t$ uses only earlier days, so no future
information enters.

\begin{algorithm}[ht]
\caption{Rolling \emph{z}-score anomaly detection (no lookahead).}
\label{alg:zscore}
\begin{algorithmic}[1]
\Require return series $r_1,\dots,r_T$; window $w$; threshold $k$
\Ensure flag $a_t$ for each day, with the quantities behind it
\For{$t \gets w+1$ \textbf{to} $T$}
  \State $W \gets (r_{t-w},\dots,r_{t-1})$ \Comment{strictly earlier days}
  \State $\mu_t \gets \mathrm{mean}(W)$;\quad $\sigma_t \gets \mathrm{std}(W)$
  \State $z_t \gets (r_t - \mu_t)\,/\,\sigma_t$
  \State $a_t \gets \big(|z_t| > k\big)$
\EndFor
\State \Return for each flagged $t$, the tuple $(z_t,\mu_t,\sigma_t,w,k)$ \Comment{exposed to the explanation engine}
\end{algorithmic}
\end{algorithm}

A small example shows why this matters. Two stocks both fall $3.2\%$ today. One has been calm
($\sigma \approx 0.40\%$), the other volatile ($\sigma \approx 1.50\%$). The same move is a clear anomaly
for the calm stock ($z \approx -8.1$) but an ordinary day for the volatile one ($z \approx -2.2$), as
Table~\ref{tab:zscore_worked} shows. A fixed percentage threshold would flag both; the $z$-score does not,
which is exactly what we want.

\subsection{Semantic Retrieval and Impact Measurement}
\label{sec:met_correlation}

\emph{What it is for:} given a new headline, find the most similar past headlines and show what happened to
their prices. This news--market correlation engine is the heart of the work, and it combines two
techniques. \emph{Retrieval:} each headline becomes a sentence embedding with Sentence-BERT
\autocite{reimers2019sbert}, chosen (Section~\ref{sec:sota_finnlp}) because it captures meaning yet yields
directly comparable vectors, so the nearest past items are found cheaply by cosine similarity.
\emph{Impact:} for each precedent, the cumulative return is measured over fixed post-event windows
($+1$, $+3$ and $+5$ trading days), in the manner of an event study
\autocite{fama1969adjustment, brown1985daily}. Both the similarity metric and the windows are explicit,
documented choices, and together they are the methodological contribution. Impact is measured strictly
after the event, so it is an outcome, not a forecast. Algorithm~\ref{alg:retrieval} states the
retrieve-and-reuse step (the case-based-reasoning core of Section~\ref{sec:sota_cbr}).

\begin{algorithm}[ht]
\caption{Precedent retrieval and impact reporting (news trigger).}
\label{alg:retrieval}
\begin{algorithmic}[1]
\Require headline $h$; knowledge base $\mathcal{K}=\{(d_j,c_j,h_j,\mathbf{e}_j,\text{impact}_j)\}$; neighbours $k$; horizon $\tau$
\Ensure top-$k$ precedents with similarity and observed impact, and their mean impact
\State $\mathbf{q} \gets \mathrm{embed}(h)$ \Comment{same Sentence-BERT model as the KB}
\For{each case $j \in \mathcal{K}$}
  \State $s_j \gets \cos(\mathbf{q}, \mathbf{e}_j)$ \Comment{cosine over L2-normalised vectors}
\EndFor
\State $S \gets$ indices of the $k$ largest $s_j$
\State \Return $\{(d_j,c_j,h_j,s_j,\text{impact}_j[\tau]) : j \in S\}$ and $\mathrm{mean}_{j\in S}\,\text{impact}_j[\tau]$
\end{algorithmic}
\end{algorithm}

Two details of the embedding step deserve to be explicit, because each answers a question an examiner
would rightly ask. The first is what $\mathrm{embed}(h)$ actually computes. Sentence-BERT is a
\emph{bi-encoder}: a pre-trained transformer reads the headline once and outputs one contextual vector
per token, and the sentence embedding is the mean of those token vectors (mean pooling), scaled to unit
length:
\begin{equation}
\mathbf{e} \;=\; \frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n} \mathbf{t}_i,
\qquad
\hat{\mathbf{e}} \;=\; \frac{\mathbf{e}}{\lVert \mathbf{e} \rVert_2},
\label{eq:pooling}
\end{equation}
where $\mathbf{t}_1,\dots,\mathbf{t}_n$ are the token vectors of the final transformer layer. The
deployed encoder (MiniLM) is a distilled six-layer transformer producing $384$-dimensional embeddings,
small enough for free infrastructure. The bi-encoder design is what makes a knowledge base practical:
every stored case is embedded \emph{once}, and a new headline costs a single forward pass plus cheap
vector comparisons. The more accurate cross-encoder alternative, which re-reads query and candidate
together, would need one full transformer pass per (query, case) pair, which at the scale of
Chapter~\ref{chap:casestudies} means tens of thousands of passes for every incoming headline. The second
detail is the similarity itself. For embeddings $\mathbf{q}$ and $\mathbf{e}$,
\begin{equation}
\cos(\mathbf{q},\mathbf{e}) \;=\;
\frac{\mathbf{q}\cdot\mathbf{e}}{\lVert\mathbf{q}\rVert_2\,\lVert\mathbf{e}\rVert_2},
\qquad
\lVert \hat{\mathbf{q}} - \hat{\mathbf{e}} \rVert_2^2 \;=\; 2 - 2\cos(\hat{\mathbf{q}},\hat{\mathbf{e}}),
\label{eq:cosine}
\end{equation}
and the identity on the right settles the ``why cosine?'' question: on unit-length vectors the cosine
equals the dot product, and Euclidean distance is a monotone function of it, so the three natural
similarity measures induce exactly the same ranking. The choice of cosine is therefore canonical rather
than arbitrary; what matters is the L2 normalisation, which stops longer texts from looking artificially
``bigger'' than short ones.

The impact itself is read off a short window after the event, as Figure~\ref{fig:eventwindow} shows. The
news may arrive on a non-trading day, so the \emph{event day} is the first trading day on or after it;
the return is then accumulated from that day's close to the close $+1$, $+3$ and $+5$ trading days later.
Anchoring on the close, and looking only forward, is what keeps the measure free of lookahead: it records
what happened \emph{after} the event became actionable, never before.

Three choices fix \emph{how} impact is measured, each made for transparency as much as rigour:
\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Horizons.} Short windows ($+1$, $+3$, $+5$ days), standard for daily event studies
        \autocite{brown1985daily}; longer windows let unrelated, market-wide moves creep in.
  \item \textbf{Baseline.} The \emph{raw} cumulative return, not a market-adjusted abnormal return, because
        it is directly observable and needs no estimated market model a non-expert could not check. The
        cost, stated plainly, is that a raw return mixes the news reaction with any market-wide move on the
        same days (the confounding threat examined in Chapter~\ref{chap:casestudies}); market adjustment is
        future work.
  \item \textbf{Aggregation.} The headline figure is the \emph{mean} impact across the retrieved
        precedents, but the individual precedents are always shown too, so the investor sees the spread, not
        just one number.
\end{itemize}

One apparent inconsistency is worth resolving here, because two parts of the system measure the
aftermath of an event differently, and both are right for their job. The \emph{displayed} impact of a
precedent uses the raw return, as chosen above: it is what the investor would actually have experienced,
and it can be checked against any public price chart. The \emph{label} of the materiality-triage model
(Section~\ref{sec:met_triage}) instead uses the market-adjusted difference
$\lvert r^{\mathrm{tkr}} - r^{\mathrm{mkt}} \rvert$, because a training target must isolate the
ticker-specific move from market-wide swings; otherwise the model would learn to predict the index
rather than the consequences of the news. It is the same event-study machinery answering two different
questions: ``what happened to this stock?'' for evidence shown to a person, and ``did this stock move
for its own reasons?'' for a label a model learns from.

\subsection{Materiality Triage: a Trained Severity Model}
\label{sec:met_triage}

\emph{What it is for:} to rank the incoming news stream, so that the user's limited attention goes to
the headlines most likely to matter (Section~\ref{sec:sota_triage}), answering \gls{RQ}4.

The unit is one (headline, ticker, event day) triple, where the event day $d$ is the first trading day
on or after the news date, the same alignment rule the knowledge base uses. The label asks a
direction-free question by construction: did an abnormal move of unusual size follow? Formally, the
example is \emph{material} when $|r^{\mathrm{tkr}}_{(d,\,d+h]} - r^{\mathrm{mkt}}_{(d,\,d+h]}| \ge \tau$.
The quantity inside the absolute value is the ticker's return over the $h$ trading days after the
event minus the market's (an index proxy): the market-adjusted outcome of
Section~\ref{sec:sota_eventstudy}. The primary setting is $\tau = 2\%$ at
$h = 3$; a sensitivity grid over $\tau \in \{1.5\%, 2\%, 3\%\}$ and $h \in \{1, 3, 5\}$ is reported so
the conclusion does not hinge on one threshold. Labels are produced by the system's own event-study
code; no manual annotation is involved.

Features respect a strict timing convention, all computable at the close of day $d$, before the label
window opens: the pre-event volatility (standard deviation of the twenty daily log-returns ending the
day \emph{before} the event), the five-day momentum to the same point, the event-day return itself
(known at the close of $d$), the headline length, a sector indicator, and the sentence embedding of the
headline. A unit test enforces the convention by mutating prices \emph{after} the event window and
asserting that no feature changes while the label does.

Model families span the interpretability--capacity trade-off of Section~\ref{sec:sota_triage}: an
always-alert floor, a volatility-only logistic regression (the strong naive rule any reviewer would ask
about), logistic regressions on context features only, on text features only, and on both together
(the main interpretable model), and a gradient-boosted ensemble \autocite{friedman2001gbm} as the
capacity ceiling.

The interpretable member of that family deserves its mathematics in full, because the deployed
explanations decompose it term by term. With standardised features $\tilde{x}_1,\dots,\tilde{x}_m$, the
logistic regression scores an example as
\begin{equation}
u \;=\; b_0 + \sum_{j=1}^{m} w_j\,\tilde{x}_j,
\qquad
p_{\text{raw}} \;=\; \sigma(u) \;=\; \frac{1}{1+e^{-u}},
\label{eq:logistic}
\end{equation}
so each product $w_j\tilde{x}_j$ is an \emph{exact} additive contribution to the log-odds $u$: no
surrogate model and no approximation, the property that makes the alert's ``why'' line faithful by
construction. Because a discriminative score is not automatically an honest probability, a Platt
calibration is fitted on the validation block only,
\begin{equation}
p \;=\; \sigma\!\big(a\,p_{\text{raw}} + c\big),
\label{eq:platt}
\end{equation}
with just two scalars $(a, c)$. The parametric form is a deliberate choice over the non-parametric
alternative, isotonic regression: a two-parameter sigmoid cannot overfit a modest validation block,
whereas isotonic regression needs substantially more calibration data before its extra flexibility pays
off \autocite{niculescu2005calibration}. An isotonic comparison on the full corpus is noted as future
work rather than assumed either way.

the embedding dimensions (when the text variant is used) are grouped into a single ``headline content''

Deployment is deliberately conservative. The score enters the alert pipeline off by default, behind a
configuration gate; the deployed variant uses context features only, so it runs on the light software
stack, and the alert text names the variant used. Once live, every triage decision is logged, and a
post-validation step later labels each matured decision with what actually happened, using the same
label rule, yielding live precision and calibration monitoring and fresh training data. This is
continual learning with delayed labels, not reinforcement learning: there is no action--environment
feedback loop, because the system's alerts do not move the market.

\subsection{Explanation Techniques}
\label{sec:met_xai}

\emph{What it is for:} to turn the computed objects into a message the investor can check. Explanation is
pursued by transparent design (Section~\ref{sec:sota_xai}) \autocite{rudin2019stop, arrieta2020xai}. Each
alert is assembled from three transparent ingredients: the rule that fired (the $z$-score with its window
and threshold); the retrieved precedents with their observed impact; and, optionally, feature attribution
over the detector with SHAP \autocite{lundberg2017shap}. Financial sentiment from a domain model
\autocite{araci2019finbert} is an optional enrichment, used only if it adds defensible value. Because every
alert is rendered directly from these objects, the explanation is faithful to the computation by
construction, a property tested in Chapter~\ref{chap:casestudies}.

\section{Tools and Frameworks}
\label{sec:met_tools}

The system is built on the open-source scientific-Python ecosystem: mature, free libraries for numerical
computing, market-data access, sentence embeddings and figures. This fits the free-resources constraint and
reproducibility, since pinned dependencies and fixed seeds let the environment and results be recreated

\section{Responsible AI and Ethics}
\label{sec:met_ethics}

The work is bound by several responsible-AI commitments that follow directly from its purpose and its
audience. \textbf{No advice and no prediction:} the system performs neither price prediction nor trading
recommendation; it surfaces observed past outcomes as evidence and states this explicitly in every alert,
so that the investor is informed rather than instructed. \textbf{Transparency:} every alert exposes its
full reasoning chain, in keeping with the explainability principles of Chapter~\ref{chap:sota}, so that a
user can verify rather than merely trust the output. \textbf{Honest evidence:} only results that can be
reproduced and explained are reported, and no data, metric or citation is fabricated. \textbf{Data
stewardship:} all sources are used within their free tiers and licences, the FNSPID dataset is attributed
as its Creative Commons licence requires \autocite{dong2024fnspid}, third-party news text is not
republished, and no personal data is processed. \textbf{Security:} credentials are never stored in
versioned files. Finally, the system is positioned as a decision-support aid for a non-professional
investor, not as a substitute for professional advice, a limitation stated plainly rather than
hidden.

\section{Evaluation Methodology}
\label{sec:met_evaluation}

\emph{How is the system judged?} By a plan fixed before any experiment ran, so the metric cannot be chosen
after the fact to flatter the result, a small-scale pre-registration. The results are in
Chapter~\ref{chap:casestudies}; here is the protocol they follow.

\subsection{Retrieval metric and relevance}
\label{sec:met_eval_retrieval}

Retrieval is evaluated as an information-retrieval task (Section~\ref{sec:sota_ir}) with precision@$k$, the
share of the top $k$ precedents that are relevant. Writing $Q$ for the set of query headlines and $R_k(q)$
for the top $k$ items returned for query $q$,
\begin{equation}
\text{precision@}k = \frac{1}{|Q|}\sum_{q\in Q}\frac{1}{k}\sum_{d\in R_k(q)} \mathrm{rel}(q,d),
\qquad
\mathrm{rel}(q,d)=\mathbf{1}\big[\,\mathrm{sector}(d)=\mathrm{sector}(q)\,\big].
\label{eq:precatk}
\end{equation}
Relevance is approximated by \emph{sector agreement}: a precedent counts as relevant if its company is in
the query's sector. This is an automatic stand-in for ``analogous'', used because no labelled set exists;
its limits are stated with the results. Two choices make the metric demanding, not flattering. First, a
strict \emph{cross-ticker} constraint drops every precedent sharing the query's ticker, so the system
cannot win by matching a company to its own past news; it must generalise \emph{across} companies in a
sector. Second, $k$ is small (the headline figure uses $k=5$), reflecting that an alert shows only a few
precedents.

\subsection{Baselines}
\label{sec:met_eval_baselines}

A single number means little alone, so semantic retrieval is compared against three baselines, each
controlling for a rival explanation. \textbf{Random} draws $k$ precedents at random; its precision is the
sector base rate, the no-skill floor. \textbf{Recency} returns the newest items, testing whether simply
surfacing fresh news would do as well. \textbf{Lexical} ranks by word overlap under the same cosine, so the
gap to the embedding model isolates the value of \emph{meaning} over surface words. Each comparison is
repeated over five random seeds (mean with standard deviation), and two Sentence-BERT variants are tried,
so the result reflects semantic retrieval in general, not one lucky draw or one model.

\subsection{Anomaly-detection protocol}
\label{sec:met_eval_anomaly}

The anomaly detector is judged primarily by an argument that needs \emph{no label}: a good universal
detector should fire at a comparable rate across instruments of very different volatility, whereas a
volatility-blind rule cannot. Consistency is therefore measured as the range (maximum minus minimum) of
per-ticker firing rates: the smaller the range, the more uniform the detector. Only secondarily is the
detector scored against an explicit \emph{extreme-move} proxy (a daily move at or beyond the $99$th
percentile of that ticker's own returns), via precision, recall and $F_1$; because this proxy is itself
volatility-relative, it is treated as supporting rather than primary evidence. An ablation over the
estimation-window length completes the picture by exposing the detector's sensitivity to its one main
parameter. Finally, the transparent rule is challenged by a \emph{learned} detector on equal terms: an
Isolation Forest \autocite{liu2008isolation} trained causally on the same information (each day's return
and the volatility of the twenty preceding days, fitted only on an initial past segment) is scored on the
same evaluation region, so the choice of a statistical rule over a learned one is tested, not assumed.
Two extensions harden that comparison further. The Local Outlier Factor \autocite{breunig2000lof}, cited
in Chapter~\ref{chap:sota} as the density-based alternative, is evaluated under the identical causal
protocol, so the statistical rule faces \emph{two} learned detectors rather than one. And the rolling
volatility estimate is challenged by an exponentially weighted variant (RiskMetrics-style,
$\lambda = 0.94$), the first empirical step toward the GARCH family of Section~\ref{sec:sota_anomaly},
so the natural ``why not GARCH?'' question receives evidence rather than opinion.

\subsection{Triage protocol}
\label{sec:met_eval_triage}

The materiality model of Section~\ref{sec:met_triage} is evaluated under a strict temporal protocol.
Examples are split into training, validation and test blocks chronologically, by \emph{unique event day}
(70/15/15), so that all headlines from one day land in the same block, and an embargo of several trading
days is removed after each boundary so that no label window can straddle two blocks. Calibration is
fitted on the validation block only; the test block is touched once, to report. The primary metric is
the area under the precision--recall curve, whose floor is the test prevalence (an always-alert
strategy); the area under the ROC curve and the Brier score complete the picture, and a product-shaped
metric, precision within a budget of $N$ alerts per day, measures what the user actually experiences.
The metrics themselves are standard but worth fixing precisely. Sweeping a threshold $s$ over the
scores traces $\big(\mathrm{recall}(s), \mathrm{precision}(s)\big)$; PR-AUC is the area under that
curve, and a scorer with no skill earns exactly the prevalence $\pi$, which anchors every comparison.
ROC-AUC is the probability that a random positive outscores a random negative; it is reported for
completeness but not used as primary, because it rewards ranking the abundant easy negatives and so
flatters a triage model whose user only ever sees the top of the ranking. Calibration is measured by
the Brier score,
\begin{equation}
\mathrm{Brier} \;=\; \frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\big(p_i - y_i\big)^2 ,
\label{eq:brier}
\end{equation}
the mean squared gap between the stated probability $p_i$ and the outcome $y_i \in \{0,1\}$: it is low
only when the probabilities can be taken at face value, which is exactly what a user reading ``$57\%$''
deserves.
The decisive comparison is against the volatility-only logistic regression: the learned model is claimed
useful only where it beats that baseline, and the feature ablations (context only, text only, both)
attribute any gain to its source. Sensitivity over the $\tau \times h$ label grid is reported alongside.
Two further guarantees are pre-committed. Training is deterministic given the seed, and rerunning it
reproduces the stored artefacts exactly. And the result is reported whichever way it falls: if the
trained model fails to beat the volatility baseline on a given corpus, that finding stands as stated.

\subsection{Explanation, scope, and rigour guarantees}
\label{sec:met_eval_rigour}

The explanation engine is assessed for \emph{fidelity}: whether the alert text reflects the system's
actual computation. This holds by construction, because each alert is rendered directly from the computed
objects. Its \emph{usefulness} to a real investor is a separate matter, which would need a human study not
yet conducted, and is reported as a limitation rather than a claim. Because the system performs neither
price prediction nor trading, profit-based metrics are out of scope by design, not omitted by convenience.
Underpinning every experiment are three guarantees. First, \textbf{no lookahead}: any feature computed at
an instant uses only information available at or before that instant, and historical impact uses only
post-event windows. Second, \textbf{reproducibility}: random seeds are fixed, dependencies are pinned, and
every data and results figure is produced by a versioned script. Third, \textbf{honesty}: results are
reported with their caveats, and modest but genuine findings are preferred to inflated ones.
